Case study / Cogoport 3.0 research

Finding out why a freight marketplace stopped growing.

Cogoport launched its digital marketplace for ocean freight in 2017 and grew fast. By 2018, that growth was slowing. Before redesigning anything, our team needed to understand why. I led a large part of the research that shaped Cogoport 3.0, from interviewing the founders to sitting with shippers in Mumbai.

Role
Senior Product Designer, research lead for the Mumbai field study
Team
UX designers, product managers, operations and marketing
Timeline
Research phase of Cogoport 3.0, late 2018 to early 2019
Methods
Stakeholder interviews, desk research, field interviews, affinity mapping

To comply with my non-disclosure agreement, I have omitted and obfuscated confidential information. This case study is my own and doesn't necessarily reflect the views of Cogoport.

Context

A good start that was losing momentum.

Cogoport’s freight marketplace grew quickly after launch, but by 2018 the team was seeing signs that the experience was losing momentum.

Step 1 / The business

Leadership and the teams saw different products.

I asked founders and teams what the product was for and who it served. Their answers exposed different assumptions about customers and the work happening behind each booking.

Step 2 / The market

Demand was growing, supply wasn't.

The marketplace needed freight suppliers, but some saw it as a threat to their customer relationships. More demand alone would not solve that imbalance.

Step 3 / The users

Sitting with shippers.

We met five freight buyers across Mumbai, Kolkata, Chennai and Baroda over two days. An associate product manager and I headed the Mumbai research team.

Step 4 / Synthesis

Sorting what we heard.

Back together, we grouped observations by Plan, Enquire, Booking and Execution. The affinity map helped the team separate repeated behaviour from isolated requests.

Findings

What we learned.

  1. Trust matters more than price

    Every shipment is a large investment, so exporters stick with agents they know. A slightly cheaper rate from a stranger isn't worth the risk.

  2. Comparing agents is part of the job

    Shippers check 3 to 4 agents on every shipment, because each is strong on different routes. The product had to support that habit, not fight it.

  3. Shipping isn't their business

    For most small and medium businesses, logistics is a cost to manage, not where they make money. In about half of them there's no export manager, so the owner or someone else handles it on the side.

  4. Documents cause the most worry

    A lost or leaked document can cost a client, and exporters still need paper records for government checks. Their main goal is to hand the goods to the agent and get the bill of lading on time.

  5. Suppliers need a reason to join

    Without forwarders and agents on the platform, there's nothing to book. They needed to see Cogoport as a partner, not a competitor.

Outcome

A new direction for Cogoport 3.0.

The research shifted the direction from a booking tool toward a marketplace that also supports trust, documents and shipment communication.

  • Verified sign-up, so only genuine businesses could see rates. This protected suppliers and Cogoport.
  • Secure, shared documents for everyone involved in a shipment.
  • One channel for updates, instead of calls, emails and messages to each party.
  • Clear, standardised costs during planning, so decisions could be made faster.
Looking back

What this project taught me.

The most useful outcome was a shared understanding of where the business model and customers’ habits did not meet.

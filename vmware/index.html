Case study / VMware

Making VMware's documentation easier to find, use and publish.

VMware's documentation is used by more than 12 million people a year, across 150+ products and 19 languages. Over 20 months, I led the redesign of the public Docs site and the two internal tools that produce it, so that customers could find answers faster and writers could publish them with less effort.

The project in 20 seconds
Problem
Customers struggled to find answers; writers worked across disconnected publishing tools.
My role
Led the redesign of Docs, DocWorks and DocDash in a two person design team.
Key decision
Connect search and navigation improvements with a simpler publishing workflow.
Outcome
The project reported 25% more daily active users and a reduction in publishing time from four days to two.
Role
Lead Product Designer, working in a two person design team
Team
14 people across India, Bulgaria, China and the USA

Context

One content system, three products.

VMware’s Content Delivery Framework connects the public Docs site with DocWorks, where writers create content, and DocDash, where teams translate and publish it. Missing or outdated information on Docs could result from delays in writing, review, translation or publishing.

Create

DocWorks

The content management system where technical writers and subject matter experts write and review documentation in DITA or Markdown.

Publish

DocDash

The internal portal where teams manage each product's table of contents, send content for translation and publish it on release day.

Read

Docs

The public website where customers, developers, solution architects and support staff look for answers.

The problem

The problem went beyond the interface.

The brief sounded like a visual refresh. Customer feedback showed a deeper problem: search, navigation and publishing did not work together.

People searched on Google or saved PDFs when Docs failed them. Writers faced scattered feedback, repeated reviews and manual publishing steps.

Discover

Talking to the people who read the docs, and the people who write them.

I combined writer workshops, a customer survey, historical feedback, analytics and accessibility audits to understand both sides of the system.

When writers mapped their daily work, we found more review and publishing steps than the technical team’s eight stage content process showed.

Define

What we learned.

  1. Most visitors come for one specific answer

    People rarely browsed. They arrived with a question about a product and version, so when search failed, they left.

  2. Navigation buried answers under products, versions and guides

    Users think in products, versions and tasks. The table of contents often buried answers several levels deep, as the example below shows.

  3. Comparing versions was a daily task

    Admins and developers constantly compared release notes and known issues between versions, switching between tabs to do it.

  4. Accessibility gaps were blocking real people

    The audit found keyboard, contrast and labelling problems. These issues made it harder to use the site with a keyboard or assistive technology.

  5. Publishing delays affected the information readers received

    Improving review and publishing in DocWorks and DocDash would help teams keep the public documentation accurate and current.

Develop

Exploring what Docs 2.0 could be.

I used clickable concepts to help the team decide what belonged in the first release. Search, navigation and the writers’ workflow had to improve together.

We used VMware’s Clarity design system and checked the designs against findings from the accessibility audit. Docs 1.1 updated typography, spacing and navigation while work on Docs 2.0 continued.

Deliver

The redesigned Docs.

The final experience gives people a clearer route from a question to the right product, version and guide.

Homepage

Let people search before browsing the product hierarchy.

Most visitors came to Docs looking for a specific answer. The homepage needed to give them a direct way to find it.

Why this mattered

Customers told us they used Google when Docs search failed them. Browsing meant working through several levels of products, versions and guides.

What changed

We put search at the top of the homepage and simplified the route through product and version pages. Search results keep the product and version in view.

Product pages

Fewer levels to dig through

Each product now has a landing page with an overview, available versions and related products. A version page then groups every guide into clear sections, such as release notes, quick start and installation, so the eight level hunt becomes two or three clicks.

Search

Filter search results by product and version.

A useful answer depends on the product and version someone is working with. The available filters change with the product or version page the reader is viewing, so they can narrow results from that starting point.

Select any screen to enlarge it.

Known issues and release notes

Compare versions without switching tabs.

Admins and developers needed to understand which issues affected their version and whether a later release resolved them. A shared issues table, targeted filters and an issue history bring that comparison into one flow.

AI assisted discovery · vAQA

Keep follow up questions in the same conversation.

The vAQA design lets readers ask a question in their own words rather than relying only on keywords. These screens show the journey from asking a question to reading an answer and continuing in a dedicated chat with conversation history.

Mobile

Keep the documentation usable on a smaller screen.

Readers can search, choose a product and version, navigate a guide and read an article on mobile. Navigation opens in a menu that can be closed to return to the article.

DocDash 2.0 · Publishing

Bring deployment status and publishing controls together.

Writers needed clearer visibility into publication errors and fewer repetitive steps. The deployment dashboard brings content status, approval and deployment actions into one view, supported by audit logs, reusable filters and consistent status messages.

Before each release, we tested an alpha version internally, then shared a beta with volunteer writers for real world feedback before launch.

Results

What changed.

In March 2023, Docs recorded 5.34 million page views. The project also reported gains in daily use and improvements to publishing and writer workflows. These are project reported outcomes across the Docs, DocWorks and DocDash redesign.

The redesign addressed keyboard, contrast, focus and labelling problems identified in the accessibility audit.

Looking back

What this project taught me.

Readers needed relevant, current documentation, but delivering it also required changes to the writers’ review and publishing tools. This project taught me to trace a reader’s problem back through the content process. Prototypes helped the team compare options when time was limited.
